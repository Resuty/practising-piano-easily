# 01 Chords: connect the symbol, the keys and the sound

A chord is a group of pitches heard as a unit. Start with C major: its tones are C, E and G. The letter C names the root; the other tones describe its structure. A symbol is a compact instruction, not a fixed picture of one hand position.

Find C4, E4 and G4 on the keyboard. Say the names before playing. Play the tones separately, then together, and listen to what stays recognizable. Fingers 1, 3 and 5 are one possible right-hand starting point, not a universal requirement. Keep the hand relaxed and never force a stretch.

Compare C with Cm: C Eb G. Only E changes to Eb. Rather than defining major as happy and minor as sad, notice the actual changed interval. Register, melody, tempo and context also affect the impression.

In this app, the keyboard example is one possible voicing. Reading a chapter does not demonstrate mastery: use the example, listen, and try the self-test. During practice you can add missing notes and correct extra ones at your own pace. Speed, unaided answers and next-day tests are not required.

# 02 Finding notes: semitones, accidentals and octaves

White keys repeat C D E F G A B. C is immediately left of each group of two black keys. Moving to the next key of either color is a semitone; two such moves make a whole tone. E-F and B-C have no intervening black key and are already semitones.

A sharp (#) raises a written note by a semitone; a flat (b) lowers it. Find F# by locating F first and moving one key right. Find Bb by locating B and moving one key left. Printed scores may use the symbols ♯ and ♭.

On an equal-tempered piano, C# and Db share a key. They are enharmonic spellings, but they do not describe the same theoretical relationship in every context. F major is F G A Bb C D E: each letter appears once. Writing A# instead would repeat A and omit B.

Play C E G in two registers. The octave changes, but the pitch classes remain C, E and G. This app labels MIDI 60 as C4. Some keyboards call the same physical pitch C3 or C5, so compare the MIDI pitch rather than relying only on the manufacturer's octave label.

Check yourself: locate C, F, F# and Bb, and explain why E-F is not a whole tone.

# 03 Intervals: count letters, then measure semitones

An interval has two parts: its letter-number distance and its actual semitone distance. Count both endpoints. C-D-E spans three letters, so C-E is a third. Four semitones make it a major third; C-Eb spans the same letters but only three semitones, making a minor third.

For a major third above F#, first count F-G-A: the answer must be an A of some kind. Then measure four semitones to get A#. Bb uses the same key but does not spell a third above F#.

| Interval | Semitones | Example above C |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Minor / major second | 1 / 2 | Db / D |
| Minor / major third | 3 / 4 | Eb / E |
| Perfect fourth / fifth | 5 / 7 | F / G |
| Minor / major sixth | 8 / 9 | Ab / A |
| Minor / major seventh | 10 / 11 | Bb / B |
| Octave | 12 | C one octave higher |

C-D# and C-Eb share a semitone distance, but their letter spans differ: an augmented second and a minor third. Six semitones are commonly called a tritone; the spelling can make this an augmented fourth or diminished fifth.

Practice C-E, C-Eb and F#-A#. Say the starting note, the correctly spelled endpoint and the semitone distance. In interval-construction exercises, play both pitches in order; an octave requires two different registers.

# 04 Major and minor triads: one changed tone

In C E G, C is the root, E the third and G the fifth. These labels describe intervals above the root, not which fingers must play them.

A major triad has formula 1-3-5 and semitone offsets 0, 4, 7. A minor triad has 1-b3-5 and offsets 0, 3, 7. The formulas compare with the major scale on the chord's own root; they are not automatically the scale degrees of the surrounding key.

| Symbol | Tones | Semitone offsets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C | C E G | 0, 4, 7 |
| Cm | C Eb G | 0, 3, 7 |
| F | F A C | 0, 4, 7 |
| Am | A C E | 0, 3, 7 |
| D | D F# A | 0, 4, 7 |
| F# | F# A# C# | 0, 4, 7 |

Do not use “skip a white key” as a universal construction rule: D major needs F#. A diminished triad is 1-b3-b5, such as C Eb Gb; an augmented triad is 1-3-#5, such as C E G#.

Play C, keep C and G in place, and lower E to Eb for Cm. Then find Am and say A C E. Build reliable note finding before increasing speed. Any suggested fingering must remain comfortable for your hand and the surrounding music.

# 05 Inversions: the bass changes, the root does not

C4 E4 G4 and E3 G3 C4 both contain C, E and G. They are both C major, but their lowest tones differ. The root identifies the chord; the lowest sounding tone determines its current bass and inversion.

| Notes, low to high | Bass | Position | Symbol |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C E G | C | Root position | C |
| E G C | E | First inversion | C/E |
| G C E | G | Second inversion | C/G |

A seventh chord also has third inversion, with its seventh in the bass, such as Cmaj7/B. Consider both hands together: right-hand E G C above a lower left-hand C is still root position overall.

Spacing and inversion are different. C3 G3 E4 is a spread voicing but remains root position. Duplicating C in another octave does not change the chord identity. Chord practice may allow these octave changes, whereas score practice requires the written pitches.

Not every slash chord is an inversion. In C/D, D is not a member of the C triad. Preserve the requested bass and interpret the harmony in context. When practicing C/E or C/G, the correct pitch-class collection alone is insufficient if the lowest note is wrong.

# 06 Seventh chords: distinguish maj7, 7 and m7

Adding B to C E G gives Cmaj7; adding Bb gives C7. That semitone difference changes the chord type. A seventh chord normally contains a root, third, fifth and seventh.

| Symbol type | Formula | Example on C |
| --- | --- | --- |
| maj7: major seventh | 1 3 5 7 | C E G B |
| 7: dominant-seventh structure | 1 3 5 b7 | C E G Bb |
| m7: minor seventh | 1 b3 5 b7 | C Eb G Bb |
| mMaj7: minor-major seventh | 1 b3 5 7 | C Eb G B |
| m7b5: half-diminished seventh | 1 b3 b5 b7 | C Eb Gb Bb |
| dim7: diminished seventh | 1 b3 b5 bb7 | C Eb Gb Bbb |

Learn the first three before memorizing the whole table. Cmaj7 and C7 differ in the seventh; C7 and Cm7 differ in the third. Bbb means B lowered twice: it shares a piano key with A, but the seventh-letter spelling matters.

A dominant-seventh *structure* does not prove dominant *function*. In F major, C7 commonly functions as V7 and leads to F; another context can give it another role.

Practice Cmaj7-C7-Cm7, moving only the changed tone. Say “B to Bb,” then “E to Eb.” Listen to the tones separately as well as together. Compare Cm7b5 with Cdim7 only after the basic three are comfortable.

# 07 Diatonic chords: from a scale to I, ii and V

To build basic chords within a key, stack alternate scale notes. In C major, start at D and take D F A: Dm. It starts on degree two, so its Roman numeral is ii; lowercase indicates a minor triad. A diminished triad adds the symbol °.

| Degree | I | ii | iii | IV | V | vi | vii° |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C-major triads | C | Dm | Em | F | G | Am | Bdim |
| F-major triads | F | Gm | Am | Bb | C | Dm | Edim |
| F-major sevenths | Fmaj7 | Gm7 | Am7 | Bbmaj7 | C7 | Dm7 | Em7b5 |

The relationships survive transposition. In F major, iii7 starts on A and contains A C E G: Am7. A numeral is not permanently tied to one chord name.

In common tonal contexts, tonic suggests a stable center, dominant often points toward tonic, and predominant chords such as ii and IV prepare the dominant. These are useful descriptions, not compulsory routes for every song.

Play C-F-G-C, then Dm-G7-C, often written ii-V7-I. Listen separately to B moving toward C and F toward E. In the harmony page, try Fmaj7 Gm7 C7 Fmaj7 in F major. Distinguish Roman numerals, which describe a chord's relation to a key, from 1-3-5, which describes a chord's internal formula.

# 08 Minor keys and chromatic tones: E7 in A minor

A natural minor is A B C D E F G. Its diatonic triads are Am Bdim C Dm Em F G. Real minor-key music also commonly uses E or E7 before Am.

Changing G to G# brings it a semitone below A, creating a leading tone. E G# B forms a strong dominant triad in A minor; adding D makes E7. A harmonic minor, A B C D E F G#, illustrates this raised seventh. Actual music can use G natural and G# according to context: not every G in a minor-key piece must be raised.

This app's basic natural-minor list uses i, ii°, III, iv, v, VI, VII. Sources comparing everything against a major-scale reference may instead write bIII, bVI and bVII. Check the notation convention.

In C-A7-Dm-G7, A7 contains chromatic C#. It commonly functions as the secondary dominant V7/ii, temporarily pointing to Dm. This slash indicates a harmonic target, unlike the bass slash in C/E.

In C-Fm-C, Fm contains Ab and can be understood as iv borrowed from parallel C minor. A chromatic chord is not automatically an error, and one such chord does not prove a modulation. Listen to E7-Am, then inspect C A7 Dm G7 in the analysis page; treat the suggested explanations as candidates.

# 09 Added tones, suspensions and extensions

Read a longer chord symbol in parts: root, basic structure, and added or altered tones. A longer name is not automatically a more useful chord.

| Symbol | Tones | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Csus4 | C F G | Replace the third with the fourth |
| Csus2 | C D G | Replace the third with the second |
| Cadd9 | C E G D | Keep the third and add a ninth |
| C6 | C E G A | Add a sixth |
| C9 | C E G Bb D | Dominant seventh plus ninth |
| Cmaj9 | C E G B D | Major seventh plus ninth |
| Cm9 | C Eb G Bb D | Minor seventh plus ninth |

A ninth is a second above an octave; an eleventh corresponds to a fourth and a thirteenth to a sixth. Their pitch classes relate to 2, 4 and 6, while octave placement belongs to voicing.

A theoretical C13 collection can be C E G Bb D F A. Practical voicings often omit selected tones because of register, melody and other instruments. E, Bb and A are often useful priorities, but this is not a universal recipe or permission to omit arbitrary notes. The app uses explicit practical-omission rules in chord checking.

Alterations are written explicitly: G7b9 contains Ab; G7#9 contains A#. Spell the latter as A#, not Bb, because a ninth above G is an A of some kind. First compare Csus4, Cadd9 and C9: does the chord retain E, and does it include Bb? Add more complex extensions only when these distinctions are clear.

# 10 From four chords to your own practice passage

Begin with C-G-Am-F, or I-V-vi-IV in C major. Play the correct chords before choosing how to connect them. Voice leading describes how individual pitch lines move between harmonies.

Compare root-position chords with these concrete voicings, listed low to high:

| Symbol | Voicing | MIDI pitches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C | C3 E3 G3 | 48 52 55 |
| G/B | B2 D3 G3 | 47 50 55 |
| Am | A2 C3 E3 | 45 48 52 |
| F/A | A2 C3 F3 | 45 48 53 |

From C to G/B, G3 stays while C3 moves to B2 and E3 to D3. From Am to F/A, A2 and C3 stay while E3 rises to F3. Listen to the bass alone, then add the upper tones.

Enter the MIDI numbers into Voice Leading to compare movement, largest leap and retained tones. These are measurements, not artistic ratings. With unequal voice counts, the current comparison matches only pairable voices and does not judge the whole arrangement.

In Composition, enter C, G/B, Am and F/A, each lasting four beats at tempo 80, then export MIDI. Reimport it in Song Analysis. A collection such as C E G A can have more than one interpretation, including C6 and Am7/C; confirm candidates using bass, melody and context.

Change one thing at a time: first an inversion, then perhaps duration. Say the chord tones, locate the keys, play slowly and compare by ear. Keep a note of the specific difficulty rather than rereading everything. Finally transpose I-V-vi-IV to F-C-Dm-Bb in F major. Hints and corrections remain available throughout.
